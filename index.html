ARA-290
protection, minus the red cells

Scientists took the healing half of the EPO hormone and left the blood-doping half behind. The result protects nerves and tissue in early trials — without raising red blood cell counts. Here is the evidence file.

Quick read

ARA-290 is an 11-amino-acid peptide engineered from a piece of erythropoietin (EPO) — the hormone famous for boosting red blood cells. Researchers discovered EPO also protects tissue and nerves through a separate receptor; ARA-290 triggers only that receptor, skipping the red-cell effect entirely. Small human trials in nerve-pain conditions show intriguing signals, including regrown nerve fibers. It remains research-stage. Scroll for the full picture.

01The peptide

What ARA-290 actually is

EPO does two jobs: make red blood cells and protect damaged tissue. ARA-290 is the second job, bottled on its own.

Eleven beads, all highlighted: the whole peptide is the active piece — a surface fragment of EPO’s helix B that docks with the repair receptor only.

EPO’s better half

Erythropoietin is famous for blood doping. Less famous: it also shields nerves and tissue from damage — through a different receptor.

11 links, one job

ARA-290 copies the helix B surface of EPO — the part that docks with the repair receptor. Eleven amino acids, engineered for stability.

The separation trick

The whole point: tissue protection without extra red blood cells — dodging the clotting and stroke risks that come with EPO itself.

Plain English

Imagine EPO as a Swiss Army knife — ARA-290 is scientists snapping off just the tiny screwdriver (tissue repair) and leaving the blade (blood boosting) in the drawer.

02Origin story

Splitting EPO in two

The tissue-protective half of a famous hormone, separated from its infamous half — and tested in people with nerve pain.

Splitting EPO in two: keep the healing, ditch the doping.
Blood doping’s shadow

EPO’s fame is athletic cheating — extra red cells, thicker blood, real danger. Any EPO-derived drug had to escape that shadow.

Two receptors, two fates

Classic EPO receptor: more red cells. The EPOR/CD131 pair: repair signals. Same hormone, different docking station, different outcome.

Why nerves first

Small-fiber neuropathy — burning pain from dying nerve endings — has few treatments, so even a hint of regrowth drew attention.

08Potential benefits

Potential benefits

The best early human signals of any peptide on this site — still small, early, and unconfirmed. The benefits file, strictly tiered.

Established scientific data

What is genuinely established — modest, and mostly preclinical.

  • Named receptor, defined target. ARA-290 activates the EPOR/CD131 innate repair receptor — shown in cells and animals. A docking station, not a guess.
  • Consistent animal tissue protection. Protection across multiple injury models — the foundation everything rests on.
  • No red-cell effect. The design goal held up in small human trials — EPO’s blood half successfully left behind, without the clotting risks that come with it.
  • Calmed inflammatory signaling. Anti-inflammatory signals in preclinical models — the repair story’s second half.

Think of it this way: EPO is a group text to the whole body — make blood AND protect tissue. ARA-290 is a direct message to one recipient: the repair receptor. The message gets through. Whether it changes the outcome in people is still being tested.

  • Diabetic neuropathy pilots. Early pilot signals suggest benefit — nowhere near conclusive.
  • The ceiling, honestly. Protection means surviving injury, not rebuilding what is gone — keep expectations at the level of the evidence.

10Glossary

Understanding ARA-290 Terminology

Eleven terms, each with the scientific definition and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceARA-290 is an 11-amino-acid peptide engineered from the outer surface of erythropoietin's helix B. It selectively activates the innate repair receptor — the EPOR/CD131 heterodimer — which mediates EPO's tissue-protective and anti-inflammatory effects. Unlike full-length EPO, it does not activate the EPOR/EPOR homodimer that drives red blood cell production, so it was designed to separate healing signals from blood-building ones. Its main human testing was small phase 2 studies in small-fiber neuropathy. It is an investigational research compound that has not been approved as a medicine by any regulator.

Plain EnglishThink of EPO as a tool with two jobs: making blood and protecting tissue. Scientists copied just the small patch of the tool that does the protecting part. The result is ARA-290: an 11-piece chain that presses the repair button without touching the blood-making button. It was tested in people with nerve pain in small trials, but it is still experimental. No regulator has approved it as a medicine.

The scienceErythropoietin is a glycoprotein hormone produced mainly by the kidneys in response to low blood oxygen. Its classic role is driving red blood cell production: it binds pairs of EPO receptors on bone-marrow precursor cells, signaling them to survive, divide, and mature into red cells. Research also showed EPO protects tissues — nerves, heart, and kidney cells — through a different receptor pairing, the EPOR/CD131 repair receptor. Its blood-building effect made it infamous as a doping agent in endurance sports. Pharmaceutical EPO remains an approved treatment for certain anemias.

Plain EnglishEPO is a hormone your kidneys release when oxygen runs low, and its main job is telling your bone marrow to make more red blood cells. That same hormone turned out to also shield injured tissue through a different docking station. Its blood-building power made it a famous cheating drug in cycling. Doctors still use lab-made EPO for certain anemias.

The scienceHelix B is one of the four alpha-helical segments that make up the folded structure of erythropoietin. Its outward-facing surface is the part of EPO that contacts the innate repair receptor (EPOR/CD131), rather than the receptor pair driving red-cell production. ARA-290 copies an 11-amino-acid stretch of this surface, which is how it activates the repair signal without triggering red-cell growth. In short, helix B is the repair face of the EPO molecule. That separation is the entire design logic behind ARA-290.

Plain EnglishPicture EPO as a folded bundle with four spiral arms. One of those arms — helix B — is the face that docks with the repair receptor. ARA-290 is a copy of eleven links from that face, which is why it triggers repair signals only. The whole drug design is just this: carve off the useful face and leave the rest behind.

The scienceThe innate repair receptor is a cell-surface receptor pairing made of one EPO receptor subunit (EPOR) plus one CD131 beta subunit. When engaged by EPO — or selectively by ARA-290 — it switches on tissue-protective, anti-inflammatory survival signaling inside the cell. It is distinct from the EPOR/EPOR homodimer, which only drives red blood cell production. This split explains how one hormone can both build blood and calm injured tissue. The repair receptor is the pharmacological target ARA-290 was built for.

Plain EnglishCells have two different EPO docking stations. One says make more blood; this other one says calm down and survive. The repair receptor is the second one — a pairing of two parts, EPOR and CD131. ARA-290 was built to press only this button. It is the entire reason the drug exists.

The scienceCD131 is a receptor subunit, formally the beta common subunit, that pairs with several different receptors in immune and repair signaling. In the repair context, it joins with EPOR to form the innate repair receptor — without CD131, that pairing cannot exist. It is also the shared subunit of the interleukin-3, interleukin-5, and GM-CSF receptors. Its presence at the EPOR complex is what routes the signal toward tissue protection rather than red-cell production. Because it is shared across multiple cytokine pathways, its biology extends well beyond EPO.

Plain EnglishCD131 is a molecular adapter that clicks together with other receptor parts. Pair it with EPOR and you get the repair receptor; without it, that receptor cannot form. It moonlights in several immune signal systems too. Its presence is what flips EPO's message from blood to repair.

The scienceNeuropathy is the general term for damage to or dysfunction of peripheral nerves, the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. Typical symptoms include numbness, tingling, burning pain, weakness, or loss of sensation, most often starting in the hands and feet. Causes range from diabetes and autoimmune disease to infections, toxins, and chemotherapy. Diagnosis combines symptom patterns with nerve conduction tests and, for small fibers, skin biopsy. Treatment focuses on the underlying cause and symptom relief, because reversing established nerve damage remains difficult.

Plain EnglishNeuropathy means damaged nerves, and it usually shows up as numbness, tingling, or burning pain in the hands and feet. Many things can cause it: diabetes, infections, toxins, or autoimmune disease. Doctors test for it with symptom patterns, nerve conduction tests, and sometimes skin biopsies. Treatment usually manages the cause and the pain, because regrowing damaged nerves is still very hard.

The scienceSmall-fiber neuropathy is nerve damage limited to the thin sensory nerve endings near the skin — the small fibers that carry pain, temperature, and autonomic signals. Its hallmark is burning or stabbing foot and hand pain, sometimes with loss of temperature sensation, while routine nerve conduction tests often look normal. Diagnosis usually requires a skin biopsy that counts nerve fiber density. It can arise in diabetes, autoimmune conditions like sarcoidosis, and sometimes for no clear reason. Treatment options are limited, which is why it became ARA-290's clinical trial target.

Plain EnglishThis is damage to the tiniest nerve endings near the skin — the ones that carry pain and temperature signals. It causes burning or stabbing pain in the feet and hands, yet standard nerve tests often look normal. Diagnosis usually needs a skin biopsy that counts the nerve tips. There are few good treatments, which is why researchers tried ARA-290 on it.

The scienceSarcoidosis is an inflammatory disease in which the immune system forms small clumps of cells called granulomas inside organs, most commonly the lungs and lymph nodes. The cause is unknown; the disease can resolve on its own or become chronic. Small-fiber neuropathy is a frequent and difficult complication, causing burning nerve pain even when the granulomas are quiet. Because it combines inflammation with nerve damage, sarcoidosis became the patient population for ARA-290's phase 2 trial. The disease's unpredictability makes clinical trials in it especially challenging.

Plain EnglishSarcoidosis is a disease where the immune system forms tiny clumps of cells in organs, most often the lungs. Nobody knows what causes it, and it can disappear on its own or linger for years. It often brings small-fiber nerve damage with it — burning pain even when the rest of the disease is quiet. That combination made its patients the test group for ARA-290's trial.

The scienceNerve fiber density is a measured count of nerve endings in tissue — typically from a small skin biopsy or a corneal scan — used to quantify nerve loss or regrowth. Because it counts physical fibers rather than asking about symptoms, it serves as an objective readout of whether nerves are actually recovering. In ARA-290's trials, increased fiber density was the headline finding suggesting regrowth. The measure is also used diagnostically: low density confirms small-fiber neuropathy. It remains a research-grade endpoint rather than a routine clinical test.

Plain EnglishInstead of asking patients how their pain feels, researchers can count the actual nerve endings in a skin sample or eye scan. More fibers means nerves may be growing back — a physical fact, not a feeling. In the ARA-290 trials, fiber counts went up, which was the headline result. It is still mainly a research measurement, not a routine clinic test.

The scienceTissue protection describes biological processes that shield cells from death after injury — by calming inflammation, blocking suicide signals, or stabilizing stressed cells. EPO's lesser-known role is activating exactly these survival programs through the innate repair receptor. ARA-290 was engineered to keep only this protective arm of EPO's activity, discarding the red-cell-building arm. Animal work shows such protection across nerves, heart, and kidneys. Whether the effect translates into a human medicine remains unproven.

Plain EnglishTissue protection means keeping injured cells alive — calming inflammation and blocking the cell's self-destruct program. It is the half of EPO's job that ARA-290 kept. Animal studies show this kind of protection in nerves, heart, and kidneys. Whether it works in people is still unproven.

The sciencePhase 2 trials are mid-stage human studies, run after initial safety testing, that ask whether a drug actually works in patients with the target disease. They are typically small — dozens to a few hundred participants — and test early signals of benefit alongside side effects. Most drug candidates fail here: what worked in animals often does not move human outcomes. ARA-290's published human data came from small phase 2 studies, which is why its results are intriguing but unconfirmed. Larger phase 3 trials would be needed to prove the case.

Plain EnglishDrug testing happens in stages, and phase 2 is the middle one: small studies in actual patients to see if the drug works. Dozens to a few hundred people, looking for early signs of benefit. This is where most promising drugs fail. ARA-290's human results come from this stage — interesting, but far from proven.
